Word origins

Why it's spelled that way

English spelling looks random until you see where a word has been. Every Daily Spelling word comes with a short origin note — the history is the hint.

Today's note

Why embarrass has a double r and a double s

  1. barraItalian: a bar
  2. imbarazzareItalian: to hinder
  3. embarrasserFrench: to block
  4. embarrassEnglish, 1600s

To embarrass someone was originally to block them — to put a bar in their way. The double rr is that bar itself, carried over from the Italian barra. The double ss comes from the French verb embarrasser, which English borrowed in the 1600s. Only later did the meaning drift from blocking someone's path to making them go red.

Memory hook: two red cheeks, two shy smiles — rr and ss.

Don't mix it up with harass, which has only one r. It comes from a different root: Old French harer, to set a dog on someone. Different family, different spelling.

separate

From Latin separare: se- “apart” + parare “to prepare”. That par is the same one in prepare, which is why the middle letter is an a.

rhythm

From Greek rhythmos, “measured flow”. The rh is how Latin wrote an initial Greek rho — and rhyme only gained its h in the 1600s by association with rhythm.

mischievous

Built from mischief (Old French meschief, “misfortune”) plus -ous. No extra i before the ending: it's three syllables, mis·chie·vous.

For teachers

Made to use in class

Daily Spelling works on a classroom projector, a shared tablet or a student's own device. There's nothing to set up and nothing for students to sign in to. A few ways teachers use it:

  1. The five-minute warm-up

    Put today's word on the board. Read the definition and example aloud, let the class suggest spellings, then use the one-letter hint to reveal it tile by tile.

  2. Friday practice test

    Students take the weekly test on their own device and mark it themselves. Nothing is recorded, so it's low-pressure practice before your real spelling test.

  3. Word detectives

    Use the origin notes to spot roots and prefixes — se- meaning “apart”, ob- turning into oc- — and hunt for other words from the same family.

  4. Spelling journals

    Students copy each word, its memory hook and a sentence of their own into a notebook. By the end of term, they've built a personal dictionary of their trickiest words.

Questions

Frequently asked

Something else on your mind? Write to hello@dailyspelling.site.

Is it really free?

Yes. The daily word, the word lists, the practice test and the email are all free, with no ads and no in-app purchases. Daily Spelling is a small independent project, and the daily word will always stay free.

Who is it for?

Students from about age 8 to 16, and the teachers and parents who help them. The word lists run from Level 1 (grades 3–4) to Level 4 (grades 9 and up), and each daily word is tagged with its level.

Is it safe for school?

Yes. There are no student accounts, no ads, no chat or comments, and students are never asked for personal information. Spelling attempts are checked in the browser and never sent anywhere. The only email address we store is one a teacher or parent chooses to give us.

How are the words chosen?

We pick words that students commonly misspell in their writing and that have an interesting reason behind their spelling. Every definition and origin note is checked against standard dictionaries before it goes up.

American or British spelling?

We use American spelling by default. Most of our words are spelled the same everywhere; when they aren't (think color and colour), we show both and explain where the difference came from.

Can I use it in my classroom?

Please do — that's what it's for. Project the daily word, use the lists for your weekly spelling, and have students take the practice test. If you share our notes publicly, a link back to dailyspelling.site is appreciated.

How do I unsubscribe from the email?

Every email has a one-click unsubscribe link at the bottom. You can also reply “stop” or write to us, and we'll remove you.
